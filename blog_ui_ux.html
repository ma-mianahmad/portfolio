UI, UX & Usability

Why these three are not the same thing, and how product teams can stop confusing them

FIGURE_4.1: USER-CENTERED_DESIGN_FRAMEWORK.IMG ZOOM: 100%

There is one mix-up I keep noticing in product teams. UI, UX, and usability are often used as if they mean the same thing. They are connected, but they are not interchangeable. When we treat them as one thing, it becomes harder to understand what is actually working and what is not.

Over time, I have found it useful to separate them into three simple ideas.

01 UI is what you see

User Interface is the visual side of a product. It includes the buttons, colors, typography, spacing, and layout people interact with on the screen.

UI shapes the first impression. A clear and well-designed interface helps people feel oriented and comfortable. They can see where to look and what to do next.

But UI has limits. Something can look polished and modern and still be confusing underneath. A nice interface does not always mean an easy experience.

02 UX is how things flow

User Experience is about how the product works as a whole. It is the structure and flow behind the screens.

It includes how users move from one step to another, how information is presented, and how decisions are guided along the way. When UX is working well, things feel smooth and predictable. People do not have to stop and figure things out at every step.

It is less about individual screens and more about the journey from start to finish.

03 Usability is how well it works

Usability focuses on what happens when real people use the product.

Can they complete their task? Do they understand what is happening? How much effort does it take?

It is practical and measurable. It does not depend on how the product was intended to work, only on what actually happens in practice.

You can have a product that looks great and has a thoughtful flow, but still struggle with usability if people get stuck, hesitate, or make mistakes along the way.

WHY THE DISTINCTION MATTERS

When these ideas are blended together, feedback gets vague. If someone says, “this design is not working,” it is not always clear what they mean. Is it a visual issue, a flow issue, or a usability issue?

Separating UI, UX, and usability makes it easier to pinpoint the problem and respond in the right way. It also helps teams have clearer conversations. Instead of debating opinions, you can focus on specific parts of the product experience.

A QUICK EXAMPLE

Take a checkout flow. The UI is the visual design of the page, how the buttons look, and how information is arranged. The UX is the sequence of steps, from cart to payment to confirmation. Usability is whether people can complete the purchase easily, without confusion or hesitation.

If users are dropping off, understanding which layer is causing the issue makes a big difference. Sometimes the problem is visual clarity. Sometimes it is the flow. Sometimes it only becomes obvious when you watch someone actually try to use it.

THE BIGGER PICTURE

You do not need to overcomplicate this. Being more intentional about these distinctions can go a long way. It helps teams design with more clarity, give better feedback, and make decisions based on what users actually experience, not just how things look on the surface.

And in the end, that is what matters most.
